Main

We know that the viking age lasted from the late 700s to the 1100s, we know this because of the written records at the time and we also know this because of the archeological evidence that has been found, we know that the vikings were raiders, traders and settlers, we know that they traveled across Europe, the North Atlantic and beyond, and that their ships were central to all of it.

There is evidence of the longships, For example we have the Gokstad ship, which was found in a burial mound in Norway in 1880, it is about 23 meters long and 5 meters wide, its long, narrow, low shape shows what a fast, shallow hull looked like. It had a mast, 16 oar holes along each side showing that this was a 32 crew ship, this ship was classified as a karve, this is a type of boat thats used for both trade and war, this ship could carry up to 64 people and also reach top speeds of 22 kph, this was also a ship thats thought to be in service for 6 to 16 years, from about 890AD to 900AD.

Another example of a longship is the Oseberg ship. This vessel was nothing like a standard warship; it was built primarily as a ceremonial ship. Because it was designed for luxury coastal travel rather than brutal sea combat, it had less oar ports, featuring 15 holes on each side for a total crew of 32. Discovered in 1904, its delicate oak hull is famous for being covered in incredibly beautiful hand carved animal patterns. Built at around 820AD, it spent roughly 14 years navigating the Norwegian fjords

The viking age lasted for roughly 250 to 273 years, this is from 973 to 1066AD, the vikings could last this long because of their longships, their longships were a key part of their sucess because of their design they could travel far, fast, and haul lots of cargo across europe, they could use them for trading, raiding and parades.

Pictures

1. The Gokstad ship. Seen from the side, the Gokstad ship was found in a burial mound in Norway in 1880. It's about 23 metres long and 5 metres wide, and its long, narrow, low shape shows what a fast, shallow hull looked like. It had a mast, 16 oar holes along each side and a steering oar on the right hand side, so this one ship shows nearly everything we talk about on this page.

Source: Wikimedia Commons

2. Inside a hull under construction. This is part of the inside of the Havhingsten fra Glendalough (the Sea Stallion from Glendalough) while the reconstruction was still being built. A view like this shows how the ship is really a shell of overlapping planks, with the ribs fitted inside afterwards. Building the shell first and tying the ribs in later is one reason the hull was so light and could flex in waves.

Source: Wikimedia Commons

3. Lowering the sail. The Sea Stallion, a modern reconstruction of a large Viking warship, lowering its sail. One square sail drove the ship across open water, and the crew could drop it and switch to oars when the wind died, when entering a river or when coming in to land. This ship sailed from Denmark to Dublin in 2007 and came back in 2008.

Source: Wikimedia Commons

4. A knarr cargo ship. Skuldelev 1 is what's left of a knarr, a broad, deep ocean going cargo ship, on display at the Viking Ship Museum in Roskilde, Denmark. It was one of five ships sunk in Roskilde Fjord around 1070 and dug up in 1962. Compared with a longship, a knarr was wider and carried more, which is why it was the ship of choice for taking people, animals and goods to places like Iceland and Greenland.

Source: Wikimedia Commons

5. Lindisfarne. These are the ruins of Lindisfarne Priory on Holy Island, off the north east coast of England. The raid on the monastery here in 793 is traditionally seen as the start of the Viking Age. The ruins in the picture belong to a later medieval priory built on the same site, but the island's spot beside the sea shows why a raid from ships could hit so suddenly.

Source: Wikimedia Commons

6. L'Anse aux Meadows. A recreated long house at L'Anse aux Meadows, on the northern tip of Newfoundland in Canada. It's the best known and best confirmed Norse site in North America, and tree rings date Norse activity there to the year 1021. To get there, Norse sailors had crossed the North Atlantic by way of Iceland and Greenland in ships built for open water.

Source: Wikimedia Commons
